🔪 Section 2

Knives & Equipment

10–12" Cast Iron Skillet
The only pan that gets hot enough, stays hot when the steak hits it, and retains that heat evenly. Non-negotiable for this method.
Instant-Read Thermometer
The single most important tool. ThermoWorks ThermoPop (~$35) or Thermapen (~$105). Medium vs. medium well is a 5°F window — guessing will cost you the steak.
Metal Tongs (12")
For flipping and holding the steak on its edges. Never pierce the steak with a fork — you lose juice every time.
Large Spoon (metal)
For basting. You need to be able to scoop pooled butter from the pan edge and pour it continuously over the steak.
Wire Rack + Rimmed Baking Sheet
For dry brining. Elevating the steak allows air to circulate underneath, drying the surface more thoroughly for a better crust.
Heavy Oven Mitt
Cast iron handles become as hot as the cooking surface. Do not grab the handle bare — it will reach 400–500°F.
Paper Towels
For drying the steak twice: before salting and again before the pan. Surface moisture is the enemy of crust.
8–10" Sharp Chef's Knife
For slicing at the table if you choose to plate in strips. Dull knives tear the meat fibers and cause juice loss. Sharp blade, single smooth stroke.
Knife note: You do not need to prep this cut at all before cooking — it goes into the pan whole. The chef's knife is only needed at the end if you slice it. A 1.4 lb ribeye can also be served whole and carved at the table.
🔪 Section 3

Ingredient Preparation

A
The Steak
Remove from packaging. Pat completely dry on all sides with paper towels — top, bottom, and all four edges. Press firmly; you want zero surface moisture. Do not rinse it under water. Do not trim the fat cap — that rendered fat is flavor.
Common mistake: Rinsing the steak, then patting it dry and thinking you're done. Rinsing introduces bacteria risk and moisture simultaneously. Just pat dry from the package.
B
Garlic
Take 2 cloves. Place them on a cutting board. Press the flat side of a knife down on each clove with the heel of your hand until it lightly crushes — you'll hear a crack. Leave the peel on. Do not mince, slice, or peel. You want slow, controlled flavor infusion into the butter — minced garlic burns in seconds at sear temperatures.
C
Herbs
Grab 2–3 short stems (sprigs) from a bundle of fresh thyme or rosemary. Each stem is 3–4 inches long with leaves still attached. Use them whole — no stripping, no chopping. They go into the pan as-is and are discarded when you plate.
D
Everything Else
Measure butter, salt, pepper, and oil ahead of time. Stage them all on the counter near the stove before the pan goes on heat. Once that cast iron is screaming hot, there is no time to search for anything. Mise en place — everything in its place before you cook.
🧂 Section 4

Seasoning & Dry Brine

A USDA Prime ribeye at 1.4 lbs needs only salt and pepper — the cut's own marbling and beefiness carries the flavor. Do not add steak seasoning blends, garlic powder, or anything else. The aromatics come from the butter baste, not the crust.

1
Apply the dry brine — 45 min to 8 hours before cooking
Pat steak dry. Hold your hand 6–8 inches above the steak and sprinkle ¾ tsp kosher salt evenly across each flat side, plus a light dusting on all four edges. It should look like more salt than feels right — that's correct. Place on a wire rack over a sheet pan, uncovered. Leave at room temperature if cooking within 45–60 min. Refrigerate uncovered if brining for longer.
The science (Serious Eats / AmazingRibs): Salt draws moisture to the surface within the first few minutes via osmosis. Within 40–60 minutes, that brine is reabsorbed into the meat, carrying salt deep into the interior and leaving the surface drier than before. The result: seasoning that penetrates past just the crust, and a drier exterior that browns faster and harder.
2
Black pepper — applied right before the pan
Season both sides with ½ tsp coarse black pepper per side just before cooking — not during brining. Pepper can turn bitter if left on too long before high heat. Freshly cracked from a mill is meaningfully better than pre-ground.

No marinade is needed or recommended for Prime ribeye. Marinades are for tougher or leaner cuts that benefit from acid tenderization. A Prime ribeye already has superior tenderness and fat content — marinating it dilutes the flavor and softens a texture that should be firm and juicy.

🔥 Section 5

Cooking Method

Method: Cast iron sear + butter baste. At 1.4 lbs and roughly 1.25–1.5" thick, this steak is well-suited to a high-heat stovetop sear. This is the same method used by Kenji López-Alt (Serious Eats / The Food Lab) for bone-in and boneless ribeyes of this profile.

Why not the grill? A grill works well for this steak, but cast iron gives you more control over internal temperature, allows for butter basting (which you cannot do on a grill), and produces a more consistent crust for a first-time cook. Master this first.
Stove Heat Level
High — 8–9 out of 10. You want the pan screaming. If your stove runs hot, 7–8 may suffice; watch for smoking.
Preheat Time
4–5 minutes, dry (no oil). The pan needs to reach ~450–500°F before anything touches it.
Pan-Ready Visual Cue
A drop of water flicked into the pan should flash-evaporate instantly with a sharp sizzle. Slight wisps of smoke rising from the surface. Not just warm — genuinely hot.
Cooking Times
Side 1: 3 min. Side 2: 2.5 min. Edges: 30–45 sec each. Baste: 60–90 sec. Total pan time: ~9–10 min.
Signs of undercooking: Interior reads below 130°F; center looks bright red rather than pink when probed. Return to pan, baste another 30–60 sec, check again.

Signs of overcooking: Interior reads above 150°F; center is grayish-brown with little pink. Unfortunately this cannot be undone. Prevention: pull at 133–135°F, not 140°F.

🍳 Section 6

Step-by-Step Cooking Instructions

1
Preparation — 45–60 min before cooking
Pat steak dry. Apply dry brine (salt only, ¾ tsp per side). Place on wire rack, uncovered. If cooking in 45–60 min, leave at room temperature. If brining longer, refrigerate. Pull from fridge 30 min before cooking. Stage all equipment and ingredients on the counter.
2
Preheat the cast iron — 4–5 minutes
Place dry cast iron on largest burner, heat on high. No oil yet. Let it heat a full 4–5 minutes. It will begin to smoke lightly — that's your signal. Open a window or turn on the range hood now. This smoke is from any residual seasoning in the pan burning off — it's normal and stops quickly.
3
Final dry and season
Pat the steak bone-dry one final time — even if it already looks dry, do it again. Add ½ tsp black pepper per side. Add 1 tsp avocado oil to the pan, swirl immediately to coat. It will smoke on contact. Move fast.
4
Sear — Side 1: 3 minutes
Lay the steak into the pan away from you. Press it down gently with tongs to ensure full contact. Do not move it. Set timer for 3 minutes. The sizzle should be loud and aggressive. If it's quiet, the pan isn't hot enough. There will be significant smoke — this is expected.
What to look for: At ~2 min, you'll see the color change creeping up the side of the steak from the bottom — the gray-brown cooked zone rising. At 3 min, the crust should be dark mahogany. If it looks pale or blond, give it another 30 seconds.
5
Flip and sear — Side 2: 2.5 minutes
Flip with tongs. The first side should be a deep mahogany-brown. If it looks pale, leave it 30 more seconds before flipping. Sear the second side 2–2.5 min without touching it.
Why shorter? The steak is now internally warmer from the first side's cook, so heat penetrates faster. The second side will cook quicker than the first.
6
Sear the edges — 30–45 sec each
Grip the steak with tongs and hold it upright on its fat cap (the thick white edge). Press it firmly into the pan for 30–45 seconds. Repeat for the other edges. This renders the fat cap and builds crust on surfaces that would otherwise go uncooked.
7
Butter baste — 60–90 seconds
Reduce heat to medium. Add 2 tbsp butter, garlic cloves, and herb sprigs to the pan. As butter melts and foams, tilt the pan toward you so butter pools near the handle. Use the metal spoon to scoop the foaming butter and pour it continuously over the top surface of the steak. Don't stop. Keep basting the entire time — especially any pale spots. The butter will turn golden and smell nutty (beurre noisette).
What's happening: The hot butter, now infused with garlic and herb oils, simultaneously cooks the top surface and coats it in aromatic fat. This is the technique that gives steakhouse flavor at home. If butter starts smoking black, reduce heat immediately.
8
Check temperature — pull at 133–135°F
Insert thermometer probe from the side into the thickest part of the steak. Tip should be in the dead center — not near the fat cap, not near the bone. Read the temp. Pull when it reads 133–135°F. If it's at 125–130°F, continue basting 30–45 more sec and check again. Do not wait for 140°F.
9
Rest — 8 minutes minimum. Non-negotiable.
Transfer steak to a plate or wooden cutting board. Spoon any pan butter over the top. Lay a piece of foil loosely over it — don't wrap tight. Set a timer for 8 minutes. Walk away. Do not check it, cut it, or poke it. Internal temperature will rise to 140–145°F on its own during this window.
The science: During cooking, muscle fibers contract and push fluids toward the center. Resting allows fibers to relax and reabsorb those juices. Cut too early and they flood the cutting board — the steak will be noticeably drier.
10
Verify and finish
After resting, probe one final time if uncertain. It should read 140–145°F. Sprinkle a small pinch of flaky Maldon salt over the surface. Serve whole at the table, or slice against the grain into ½" strips. Either approach is correct for a ribeye.
Against the grain = cut perpendicular to the lines of muscle fiber you can see running across the steak. Cutting this way shortens the fibers and makes each bite perceptibly more tender.
🥦 Section 7

Vegetables & Side Components

A heavily marbled Prime ribeye is rich and fatty. The best sides contrast that richness — cut through the fat, refresh the palate, or provide textural contrast. Avoid competing flavors; let the steak lead.

A
Garlic butter mushrooms (stovetop — make first, keep warm)
Cremini or shiitake mushrooms, halved. Medium-high heat, 1 tbsp butter + 1 tbsp oil. Don't crowd the pan or they'll steam instead of brown. Cook 4–5 min undisturbed, flip, cook another 3 min. Add crushed garlic and thyme the last minute. Season with salt and pepper. Desired texture: deep golden-brown exterior, meaty interior. Their natural umami doubles down on the steak's savoriness beautifully.
B
Asparagus (oven-roasted — time to finish with the steak rest)
Oven at 425°F. Snap off woody ends. Toss with olive oil, salt, pepper. Spread on sheet pan, single layer. Roast 10–12 min until edges begin to char lightly. Finish with lemon juice and optional Parmesan. Desired texture: tender but with slight resistance — not limp. The acidity of the lemon cuts directly through ribeye fat and refreshes the palate between bites.
C
Creamed spinach (classic steakhouse — can be made ahead)
Wilt fresh spinach in a dry pan. In a separate saucepan: sauté minced shallot in butter, add heavy cream, reduce by half, add Parmesan and a pinch of nutmeg, fold in spinach. Season aggressively. The contrast: the richness of cream against the steak sounds like doubling up on fat, but the slight bitterness of spinach and the fresh dairy flavor cleanse between bites in a way potatoes cannot.
D
Cooking order if making all three
Start creamed spinach first (can hold warm). Put asparagus in oven 12 min before steak goes in the pan. Mushrooms in a second pan simultaneously with the steak sear, or done first and kept warm in a low oven (200°F). Plate everything as steak rests.
🔄 Section 8

Finishing Steps

1
Pan sauce (optional, restaurant-level — uses the fond)
After removing the steak to rest, the pan has brown bits stuck to the bottom — this is called fond, and it's pure concentrated beef flavor. With the pan still over medium heat, pour in 2 oz cognac or red wine and scrape the bottom with a wooden spoon. The liquid will immediately deglaze all those bits. Reduce 30 sec, add 2 tbsp butter, swirl until glossy. Pour directly over the steak at plating. This is a professional finishing technique called au jus in its simplest form.
2
Compound butter (make ahead, keep in freezer)
Mix 4 tbsp softened butter with 1 tsp minced fresh thyme, ½ tsp garlic powder, ½ tsp lemon zest, salt. Roll in plastic wrap into a log, freeze. Slice a round and place it on the hot rested steak at plating — it melts slowly across the surface. This is the signature move of French brasseries and American chophouses alike.
3
Final seasoning
After resting, taste a small piece before adding anything. If it needs more salt, use flaky Maldon — never kosher at this stage. Do not add more pepper. A few drops of good-quality red wine vinegar or a squeeze of lemon can brighten an overly rich steak without masking the beef flavor.
🍽️ Section 9

Serving Suggestions

A 1.4 lb ribeye feeds 1–2 people depending on appetite. Plate on a warmed plate — a cold plate drops steak temperature fast.

Starches
Garlic mashed potatoes (classic, soak up pan sauce), salt-crusted baked potato (steakhouse staple), or pommes frites. Avoid anything too sweet or acidic.
Vegetables
Asparagus with lemon, garlic butter mushrooms, roasted Brussels sprouts, or creamed spinach. All cut through the fat effectively.
Salads
Wedge salad with blue cheese dressing (classic steakhouse palate cleanser) or arugula with shaved Parmesan and lemon vinaigrette — the pepper bite of arugula is ideal against a fatty ribeye.
Sauces
Compound butter (on top), pan sauce/au jus (poured around), or horseradish cream (alongside). Avoid heavy cream sauces — the steak already provides richness.
Steakhouse tip: Warm your plates in a 200°F oven for 5 minutes before plating. A cold plate is one of the most common reasons a perfectly cooked steak arrives at the table lukewarm.

🚫 Section 11

Common Mistakes

Pan not hot enough
Cause:Putting the steak in before the pan reaches temperature, or skipping the preheat stage.
Result:Gray, steamed exterior with no crust. Pale, unappetizing surface.
Fix:Preheat a full 4–5 minutes until smoking. When oil hits the pan, it should smoke immediately.
Wet surface before searing
Cause:Not drying the steak, or rinsing it before cooking.
Result:Surface moisture converts to steam, dropping pan temperature and preventing browning.
Fix:Pat completely dry twice — once before salting, once right before the pan.
Pulling at the target temperature (140°F)
Cause:Not accounting for carryover cooking during the rest period.
Result:Steak overshoots to ~148–152°F — medium-well. Less pink, drier.
Fix:Always pull at 133–135°F. Carryover adds 7–10°F over the 8-min rest. This is non-negotiable.
Moving the steak constantly
Cause:Checking, poking, or repositioning the steak during the sear.
Result:Heat dissipates, surface doesn't build a continuous crust, meat sticks inconsistently.
Fix:Put it down, set the timer, don't touch it. The steak releases naturally from the pan when it's ready.
Cutting immediately after cooking
Cause:Impatience — the steak smells incredible.
Result:Juice floods the cutting board instead of staying in the meat. Noticeably drier first bites.
Fix:8 minutes minimum. Use the time to plate vegetables and sauce.
Using butter for the initial sear
Cause:Adding butter to the pan at the sear stage because it sounds good.
Result:Butter burns at ~300°F — produces black, acrid smoke and burnt flavor on the crust.
Fix:Sear in avocado/grapeseed oil only. Add butter only after reducing heat for the baste.
Not using a thermometer
Cause:Relying on the finger/touch test or visual cues alone.
Result:Inconsistent doneness. Medium vs. medium well is a 5°F window — touch testing is ±15°F.
Fix:Buy a ThermoWorks ThermoPop. $35. Use it every time, no exceptions.

✓ Signs you nailed it
  • Deep mahogany crust — no gray patches, slight char at the very edges
  • Warm pink center throughout when cut — not red, not gray-brown
  • Juices stay in the meat; only a small pool on the plate, not flooding the board
  • Rendered, silky fat cap — golden, translucent, not white and waxy
  • Nutty brown butter aroma with garlic and herb notes in the crust
