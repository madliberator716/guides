What Umrah actually is ما هي العمرة

The single most useful thing to understand before you book anything.

Umrah is a visit to the Kaaba in Makkah, performed in a specific way. It's often called the "lesser pilgrimage," to distinguish it from Hajj. And here is the fact that surprises almost everyone doing it for the first time:

The Umrah itself takes about four hours.

It is four things, in order: enter a state called ihram, walk seven circles around the Kaaba, walk seven times between two hills, and cut some hair. That's the entire rite. There is nothing else you are obliged to do.

So a seven-night trip is not seven nights of ritual. It's four hours of ritual on one afternoon, and then a week of praying in two extraordinary mosques, visiting places where things happened, and being somewhere that most Muslims spend a lifetime hoping to reach. The itinerary below is built around that reality.

Umrah is not Hajj

People conflate these constantly. They're different obligations.

  • Hajj is one of the five pillars of Islam — required once in a lifetime of any Muslim who can physically and financially manage it. It happens on fixed dates in the month of Dhul Hijjah, takes five or six days, involves standing at Arafat and camping at Mina and Muzdalifah, and draws two million people at once. You need a specific Hajj visa. You cannot do it in November.
  • Umrah is voluntary and highly rewarded — not one of the five pillars, though a minority of scholars hold it obligatory once. It can be done at almost any time of year, takes an afternoon, and involves only Makkah.

What you're doing in November 2026 is Umrah. Nothing about Hajj applies to you.

What the Kaaba is

A cube of grey stone, about fifteen metres tall, standing in the middle of an enormous mosque. Muslims hold that Ibrahim (Abraham) and his son Ismail (Ishmael) raised it as the first building on earth dedicated to the worship of one God. It's draped in the kiswah — a black silk cover embroidered with gold Qur'anic calligraphy, replaced every year.

It is the qibla: the direction every Muslim in the world faces to pray. That's the crucial distinction — it's a direction, a shared point of orientation for a global community, not an object of worship. Nobody prays to it. It's empty inside and pilgrims don't go in.

Worth internalising

Everything you'll do in Makkah is organised around that cube. The mosque was built around it. The circling is around it. Your hotel is priced by its distance from it. And the first time you walk through the gate and see it, most people find it hits considerably harder than they expected. Don't schedule anything for the hour afterwards.

What Madinah has to do with it

Nothing, technically. Visiting Madinah is not a part of Umrah. There's no ritual there, no ihram, nothing required. It's a separate visit — a ziyarah — that nearly every pilgrim makes because Madinah is the city the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ migrated to, built the first mosque in, established the first Muslim community in, and is buried in.

You are combining two distinct things into one trip, which is what almost everyone does. It's worth knowing they're separate, because it explains why Madinah has no rules attached and Makkah has many.

The words you'll keep hearing المصطلحات

Everyone around you will use these constantly and nobody will define them. Here they are once.

Ihramإحرام
A state, not a garment — though the two white sheets men wear are also called ihram. You enter it at a boundary point, and while you're in it a set of ordinary permitted things becomes temporarily forbidden. You exit it by cutting your hair.
Miqatميقات
A boundary station around Makkah. There are five, one for each direction of approach. You cannot cross one heading for Makkah to perform Umrah without already being in ihram. Yours, coming from Madinah, is Dhul Hulayfah.
Talbiyahتلبية
The pilgrim's chant, the phrase in the header above. You start it at the miqat and repeat it until you reach the Kaaba. Hearing thousands of people say it at once is one of the things people remember.
Tawafطواف
Walking seven circuits around the Kaaba, anticlockwise, with the Kaaba on your left.
Matafمطاف
The white marble floor immediately around the Kaaba where tawaf happens. When people say "the mataf is packed," this is what they mean.
Sa'iسعي
Walking seven times between two small hills, Safa and Marwa, now enclosed inside the mosque as a long marble corridor.
Halq / Taqsirحلق / تقصير
Shaving the head (halq) or trimming the hair (taqsir). This is what ends the ihram state and completes the Umrah.
Masjid al-Haramالمسجد الحرام
The mosque in Makkah built around the Kaaba. The largest in the world; holds well over a million. Usually just "the Haram."
Masjid an-Nabawiالمسجد النبوي
The Prophet's Mosque, in Madinah. The green dome you've seen in photographs is over his grave, which sits inside the mosque.
Rawdahالروضة
A small green-carpeted area inside Masjid an-Nabawi that the Prophet ﷺ called "a garden from the gardens of Paradise." Roughly 22 × 15 metres, and millions want in — hence the permit system.
Zamzamزمزم
A well beneath Masjid al-Haram that has run continuously for thousands of years. The water is free everywhere in both mosques.
Ziyarahزيارة
A visit — to Madinah, or to historical sites. Optional, not a rite.
Nusukنسك
The Saudi government app. Nothing spiritual — it's the booking system for your Umrah permit and your Rawdah slot. You cannot get into either mosque's restricted parts without it.
ﷺصلى الله عليه وسلم
The symbol written after the Prophet's name, meaning "peace and blessings be upon him." You'll see it everywhere.

How a day works over there اليوم

This itinerary is built on prayer times rather than clock times, which needs explaining if you're not used to living that way.

Muslims pray five times a day at times set by the sun, so the times shift daily and by location. In Makkah and Madinah this isn't a private matter — the entire city stops. Shops shutter for twenty to thirty minutes at each prayer, taxis pull over, restaurants stop serving. The mosques begin filling half an hour beforehand and empty slowly afterwards. Planning a day by clock time there simply doesn't work.

  • Fajr~5:15 amBefore sunrise. The best hour in both mosques — cool, full but not crushed, and the whole day feels different if you make it.
  • Dhuhr~12:10 pmJust past midday. The hottest part of the day; most people go back and sleep afterwards.
  • Asr~3:20 pmMid-afternoon. The day starts moving again after this.
  • Maghrib~5:45 pmImmediately at sunset, and the window is short. The city is at its busiest.
  • Isha~7:10 pmNight. Many people stay in the mosque between Maghrib and Isha rather than leaving and coming back.

Approximate for Makkah in November — use a prayer-time app for the real numbers each day.

The practical shape

Your usable free time is after Fajr until about 10am, and after Asr until Maghrib. Midday is for sleeping — not out of laziness but because the marble is genuinely too hot and you'll be getting up before 5am every day. Every ziyarah trip in this itinerary sits in one of those two windows.

Why this trip is shaped this way لماذا

Three decisions drive everything else. Here's the reasoning, so you can change them knowingly.

Madinah first

Fly into Madinah (MED), out of Jeddah (JED) — an open-jaw ticket

Madinah is the gentler city: smaller, cooler, calmer, and — because it isn't part of the Umrah — there's no ihram to manage on arrival. You land, sleep off the flight, and ease in. By the time you reach Makkah you're rested, acclimatised, and you've already learned how a mosque holding half a million people actually works.

It also solves the hardest logistical problem. Remember that you must be in ihram before you cross the miqat boundary. Coming from Madinah, your miqat is Dhul Hulayfah — an actual mosque ten minutes outside the city, with showers and changing rooms, where you can do this calmly. Flying into Jeddah instead means the boundary is crossed in the air, so you'd be changing into two unstitched sheets in an aircraft lavatory somewhere over the Red Sea. People do it. It's not the way to start.

The counter-argument: some prefer Makkah first, so that if illness or a delay derails the trip, the Umrah is already done. If either of you is travelling with a health concern that makes that a real risk, flip the order — everything below still works in reverse.

3 nights Madinah / 4 nights Makkah

Madinah's programme is finite: the mosque, the Rawdah, Quba, Uhud. Three nights covers it without padding. Makkah rewards time — the Haram is the trip, and you'll want unhurried days there once the Umrah itself is behind you. Four nights also gives you a spare day if the Umrah takes longer than expected, which for first-timers it usually does, or if it has to be postponed (see the note on her cycle further down — this is a real and common planning issue).

Splurge in Makkah, not Madinah

Rooms beside Masjid an-Nabawi are genuinely affordable — you can be a four-minute walk from the courtyard on a mid-range budget. Makkah is where proximity costs real money, and where it matters most: you'll walk to the Haram five times a day in 33°C heat, and the difference between a 5-minute covered walk and a 20-minute shuttle is the difference between praying there five times and praying there twice.

Spend the money on distance, not on a Kaaba view. View rooms carry a large premium, and you'll be inside the mosque for most of the hours you'd otherwise spend looking at it.

What to book, and when التحضير

Two of these have hard deadlines that catch first-timers out: the vaccine and the Rawdah slot. Everything else is flexible.

  • NowFlights. Open-jaw: into Madinah, out of Jeddah. Saudia, Emirates, Qatar and Turkish all route there; a Jeddah return has far more options than a Madinah one.
  • 4–5 months outHotels. They must be licensed and documented on a Saudi platform called Nusuk Masar — since June 2025 an unlicensed booking can block your visa outright. Book through Nusuk itself, a licensed agent, or a major international chain that handles the registration for you.
  • 8 weeks outMeningococcal ACWY vaccine. Mandatory for both of you — Saudi Arabia requires it of all pilgrims because of the crowding — and it must be given at least 10 days before arrival. Keep the certificate on your phone and on paper.
  • 6 weeks outSaudi tourist e-visa at visa.visitsaudi.com. Umrah is an approved purpose on the ordinary tourist visa, so you don't need a special pilgrimage visa. Roughly SAR 535 including mandatory health insurance; valid a year, multiple entry, 90 days per stay. Often approved in minutes.
  • 4 weeks outHaramain train tickets for the Madinah–Makkah leg. Booking opens roughly four to five weeks ahead at sar.hhr.sa or in the HHR Train app. Economy SAR 150–200; business SAR 250–300 and worth it when you're wearing two sheets.
  • 10 days outNusuk app — the one that bites. Register with passport and visa number, then book two separate things: an Umrah permit (your timed entry to Masjid al-Haram) and a Rawdah permit (your slot in Madinah). Rawdah slots release about 7–10 days ahead and vanish in seconds. Set an alarm. Book each of yours separately — men's and women's are different services with different hours.
  • 1 week outeSIM (STC, Mobily or Zain). You need working data at the mosque gates to load a QR permit. Download Nusuk, HHR Train, Careem, and a prayer-time app.

The Rawdah permit is not optional and not recoverable

No permit, no entry — security scans the QR code at the gate and there are no walk-ins, no agent workarounds, no talking your way in. You may be limited to one visit per year, so a missed slot can cost you the whole trip's chance. Screenshot the QR code as a backup; the mobile signal near the mosque is unreliable and the app is slow. There are staffed Nusuk help desks at Madinah airport and in the Nabawi courtyard if something goes wrong.

The seven days البرنامج

Anchored to prayers rather than clock times, for the reasons above.

1
Madinah

Arrive. Do almost nothing.

On landing

Clear immigration at MED, pick up an eSIM if you haven't, taxi twenty minutes to the hotel. No ihram needed — Madinah is not a miqat and Umrah hasn't begun.

Maghrib

First walk into Masjid an-Nabawi. Enter right foot first, which is the custom for any mosque. Pray two units of prayer as a greeting to the mosque, then go and give salaam to the Prophet ﷺ — you file past his grave inside the mosque, say a brief greeting facing it, and keep moving so others can pass. It takes ninety seconds and the queue does not stop moving.

Isha

Pray, then sit outside under the giant retractable umbrellas. Don't plan anything else tonight. Find which numbered gate is closest to your hotel and photograph the number — every gate looks identical and you will need this every single day.

2
Madinah

The mosque, and the Rawdah

Fajr

Leave thirty minutes early. Fajr here is the best hour of the day — cool, unhurried, and the mosque is full without being crushed.

After Fajr

Jannat al-Baqi, the cemetery beside the mosque, opens for a window after Fajr — men only. Thousands of the Prophet's Companions and family are buried there in deliberately unmarked graves. Ten quiet minutes of walking. Say a greeting to the dead; don't look for specific graves or touch anything.

Your slot

The Rawdah. Arrive thirty minutes early. Men to Gate 37, women to Gate 25. Have the QR code up and the phone unlocked. You'll get roughly ten minutes standing on the green carpet — pray two units, make whatever du'a you came to make, and don't rush anyone else.

Dhuhr → Asr

Back to the hotel and sleep. You're still on the wrong time zone and the midday sun is punishing. This nap is what makes Fajr possible tomorrow.

Maghrib → Isha

Both prayers in the mosque, staying put during the ninety minutes between them — which is how most people here actually do it. Afterwards, the date souq behind the mosque: ajwa, safawi, sukkari. They'll let you taste before you buy. Do.

3
Madinah

Out to Quba and Uhud

Fajr

In Nabawi. Then breakfast — and perform your ablutions at the hotel before you leave, specifically for Quba. See below for why.

Duha

Masjid Quba, ten minutes south. The first mosque built in Islam. There's a saying of the Prophet ﷺ that whoever purifies himself at home, comes to Quba and prays two units there, receives the reward of an Umrah — which is why you did wudu at the hotel. Recently expanded and rarely crowded in the morning.

Mid-morning

Uhud — the mountain, the martyrs' cemetery, and Archers' Hill, where a battle in year 3 of Islam turned on a small unit leaving its post. Then Masjid al-Qiblatayn, "the mosque of the two directions," where the direction of prayer changed from Jerusalem to Makkah mid-prayer and the whole congregation turned around where they stood. A Careem driver will do the loop for SAR 120–150; agree the price upfront.

Dhuhr

Back in Nabawi. Afternoon off — this is the last unstructured day before the Umrah.

Isha

Farewell salaam to the Prophet ﷺ. Then pack, and pack deliberately: ihram sheets out and on top, unscented toiletries in the washbag, everything scented sealed away in the suitcase. Tomorrow this matters.

الميقات · Dhul Hulayfah

This is where the trip changes state. Before you cross this line you are a traveller; after it you are a pilgrim in ihram, and a different set of rules applies until you cut your hair in Makkah.

The train does not stop at the miqat. It crosses the boundary roughly seven to ten minutes after leaving Madinah, with one announcement that is famously hard to hear. So you handle it on the ground, before boarding: wash and put on the ihram garments at the hotel, taxi ten minutes to Masjid Dhul Hulayfah — everyone calls it Abyar Ali — pray two units there, then make the intention and start the talbiyah. Then straight to the station.

4
Madinah → Makkah

Ihram, the train, the Umrah

Fajr

Last prayer in Nabawi. Back to the hotel, check out, leave the bags with the concierge.

Before Duha

Ghusl — a full ritual wash — then into ihram: two white sheets for him, ordinary modest clothing for her. Unscented everything from this moment. Taxi to Abyar Ali, two units of prayer, then the intention and the talbiyah aloud. You are now a pilgrim, and the restrictions are live.

Late morning

Haramain train to Makkah, 2h20m at 300 km/h. Business class is worth the extra SAR 100 in ihram — wider seats, and you can decline the scented hand towel without a scene. Keep the talbiyah going quietly the whole way.

On arrival

Makkah station is ten minutes from the Haram. Drop the bags at the hotel but stay in ihram — no showering with soap, no changing clothes. Eat something light. You have a long few hours ahead and most people badly underestimate them.

The Umrah

Enter through the King Abdulaziz Gate. The first sight of the Kaaba — stop where you are and make du'a; it's a moment people traditionally hold as one where prayers are answered. Then tawaf, sa'i, and cutting the hair. Read the next section before this day, more than once. Allow four hours; first-timers rarely take less.

After

You're out of ihram and every restriction lifts at once. Shower properly, eat properly, sleep properly. You've done it.

5
Makkah

Recover. Learn the building.

Fajr

In the Haram. Then go back and sleep — yesterday was physically much harder than it felt at the time.

Duha

Deliberately learn the mosque, because it's enormous and confusing. Which numbered gate is yours; where the women's sections are; where the escalators to the first floor and roof are; where the Zamzam coolers stand. Then agree a meeting gate, by number, for when you get separated. You will get separated.

Dhuhr → Asr

Rest through the heat. This is a light day on purpose.

After Isha

A voluntary tawaf, late. Circling the Kaaba isn't only part of Umrah — you can do it any time, as an act of worship on its own, as often as you like. Between roughly 11pm and 2am the mataf thins out and you can walk the ground floor close to the Kaaba instead of being carried along by a crowd. For a lot of people this ends up being the memory of the whole trip.

6
Makkah

Ziyarah, and an optional second Umrah

After Fajr

The Makkah loop by car. Jabal an-Nur, the mountain holding the cave of Hira where the first revelation came — view it from below unless you're both fit, because the climb is one to two genuinely punishing hours. Then Mina, Muzdalifah and Arafat, the three sites used during Hajj. Driving through an empty Arafat outside the season, seeing the plain where two million people stand for a single afternoon each year, is quietly moving. SAR 200–300 for the circuit.

Alternative

If the heat and crowds have worn you down, skip the tour and go to the Exhibition of the Two Holy Mosques Architecture instead — retired kiswah panels, the original minbar, the historic door of the Kaaba. Air-conditioned, uncrowded, genuinely excellent.

Optional

A second Umrah. Once you're inside Makkah you're past the miqat, so to perform Umrah again you have to leave and re-enter ihram at a nearer boundary — Masjid Aisha at Tan'eem, twenty minutes out. Ihram there, come back, repeat the rites. Many people do this a second time on behalf of a parent or someone who has died. Equally valid to skip it and spend the hours doing tawaf instead; scholars differ on which is better, and there's no wrong answer.

Maghrib → Isha

In the Haram. Afterwards, the Abraj al-Bait mall directly underneath the clock tower — prayer mats, attar, gifts, and air conditioning.

7
Makkah

The last full day

Fajr

In the Haram, and stay there. Don't fill this morning with errands.

Duha

Practicalities. Collect a sealed 5-litre Zamzam container from an official distribution point or at the airport — never from a street vendor, where it may not be the real thing. Airlines carry it as checked baggage and most people bring some home for family. Finish the gift shopping.

Dhuhr → Asr

Rest, repack, weigh the bags. Dates and Zamzam are heavier than you think.

Maghrib → Isha

Both prayers in the Haram, staying between them.

Late

A farewell tawaf. For Hajj this is required; for Umrah it isn't, and scholars differ on whether it's even recommended. But ending your trip with seven unhurried circuits at midnight is the right way to leave, whatever the ruling.

8
Makkah → Jeddah

Home

Fajr

Last prayer in the Haram. A final look at the Kaaba from the gate on your way out.

Morning

Haramain train straight to King Abdulaziz Airport (KAIA) — about an hour, and the station is inside the airport, so it beats the taxi comfortably. Or take an earlier train and spend a long layover in Al-Balad, Jeddah's coral-stone old town, with lunch on the corniche — a soft landing back into ordinary travel.

Compressing to seven days flat

If your ticket gives you seven days including the departure day rather than eight, cut Day 5 and fold the recovery into the afternoon of Day 4. Don't cut Day 3 — Quba and Uhud are the reason to be in Madinah beyond the mosque. And don't cut the last morning; a rushed departure sours the whole thing.

The Umrah itself, step by step المناسك

Read this before Day 4. It's four steps in a fixed order and none of it is complicated. What throws first-timers is the scale, not the ritual — so each step below explains what you do and where it comes from, because knowing why makes it far easier to do calmly.

  1. Enter ihram — the state, not just the cloth

    What you do: a full wash (ghusl), then two unstitched white sheets for him — izar wrapped below the waist, rida over the shoulders, no underwear, nothing sewn or fitted, head uncovered, sandals that leave the top of the foot bare. Ordinary modest clothes for her; there is no special garment for women. Two units of prayer at the miqat, then form the intention in your heart and say the talbiyah out loud. Keep repeating it — men audibly, women quietly — until you reach the Kaaba.

    Why the sheets

    The white cloth is deliberately levelling. In ihram a surgeon and a labourer, a Saudi prince and a Bangladeshi factory worker, are visually indistinguishable — you can't tell anyone's nationality, wealth or profession. Everyone arrives at the same place wearing the same two pieces of unsewn cloth, which is also what a Muslim is buried in. That's the entire point, and the restrictions that come with it work the same way: for a few hours you set aside grooming, scent, argument and intimacy, and are stripped down to why you came.

  2. Tawaf — seven circuits of the Kaaba

    What you do: enter the mosque, and when the Kaaba first comes into view, stop and make du'a. Go to the corner where the Black Stone sits — it's marked by a green light on the opposite wall and a line on the floor. Face it, raise your right hand toward it, say Bismillāh, Allāhu akbar, and start walking with the Kaaba on your left. Seven circuits, ending where you began. Men uncover the right shoulder throughout and walk briskly for the first three circuits; women walk normally.

    There is no set du'a for the circuits — say whatever you actually want to say, in whatever language. Between the Yemeni corner and the Black Stone, many say the Qur'anic Rabbanā ātinā fid-dunyā ḥasanah…

    About the Black Stone

    A dark stone set in a silver frame in one corner of the Kaaba, held to date to Ibrahim's construction. Its job in the rite is simply to mark where each circuit starts and stops. People do fight to reach and kiss it, and you should not join them — it's dangerous in a crowd and pointing at it from a distance is completely valid. The caliph Umar, kissing it, said plainly that he knew it was only a stone that could neither harm nor help anyone, and that he kissed it only because he'd seen the Prophet ﷺ do so. That's the correct attitude to bring.

    If the ground floor is impassable

    The first floor and the roof are entirely valid for tawaf. The circuits are much longer — a lap up there is a serious walk — but the air is calmer, nobody is pushing, and you can see the whole thing. Don't treat the upper levels as second best.

  3. Two units of prayer, then Zamzam

    What you do: pray two units behind Maqam Ibrahim — a stone in a gold and glass case near the Kaaba, held to bear Ibrahim's footprints from when he built it — if you can get anywhere near it. Anywhere else in the mosque is fine if you can't, and usually you can't. Then drink Zamzam, standing, facing the Kaaba, and make du'a. The coolers are everywhere and the water is free.

  4. Sa'i — seven trips between Safa and Marwa

    What you do: go up to Safa, face the Kaaba, and begin. Safa to Marwa is one. Marwa back to Safa is two. You finish on the seventh, at Marwa. It's a long enclosed marble corridor, roughly 3.5 km in total, and it takes most people an hour or more. Men jog the short stretch between the two green markers; women walk throughout.

    What you're re-enacting

    Ibrahim left his wife Hajar and their infant son Ismail in a barren valley with almost nothing. When the water ran out and the baby was dying, she climbed one hill to look for help, saw nothing, ran to the other, saw nothing, and did it seven times. When she returned, water was flowing from the ground at the child's feet. That spring is Zamzam — the same well, still running, now under the mosque you're standing in. The valley became Makkah because of that water.

    So sa'i is a mother's desperate search for water for her child, preserved as an act of worship for four thousand years and performed by millions of people a year. The two hills are still there; the mosque has simply been built around them.

    There are wheelchairs and electric carts available in the corridor. Using one is not a compromise and nobody will think anything of it.

  5. Cut the hair — and it's finished

    What you do: he shaves his head, or trims it evenly all over — shaving carries the greater reward, and plenty of men do it. She gathers her hair and trims a fingertip's length (about 2–3 cm) from the ends, herself or by his hand, in private. Use a licensed barber with a fresh blade rather than a curbside one; shared razors are a real bloodborne-infection risk.

    That's the whole Umrah. You are out of ihram and every restriction lifts immediately.

What's actually required, in case you're worried about getting it wrong

Only four things: ihram entered at the miqat with the intention, tawaf, sa'i, and cutting the hair. Everything else in this guide — Madinah, the Rawdah, Quba, Uhud, extra tawafs, a second Umrah, the farewell tawaf — is optional. If you do those four things in that order, your Umrah is complete, however clumsily it went.

The rules while you're in ihram المحظورات

These apply from the moment you make the intention at Abyar Ali until the moment your hair is cut in Makkah — realistically about eight hours.

Both of you

  • No cutting hair or clipping nails
  • No perfume or scented anything. This is the one people break by accident, because scent is in everything. Check your soap, shampoo, deodorant, wet wipes, lip balm and hand sanitiser before you leave Madinah, buy unscented versions at home, and pack them in a separate bag.
  • No marital intimacy, and nothing leading toward it, until after the hair is cut
  • No hunting or killing game, no arguing, no coarse or angry speech

Him

  • Two unstitched sheets only — nothing sewn, nothing fitted, no underwear, no belt with a buckle. A plain money belt is fine and worth having.
  • Head uncovered — no cap, and don't hold an umbrella pressed against your head
  • Sandals leaving the instep and ankle exposed

Her

  • Ordinary modest clothing. There is no ihram garment for women — loose, plain, comfortable, and shoes you can walk five kilometres in. An abaya is customary and easiest, not required.
  • No face veil touching the face and no gloves, on the majority view. A loose drape hung clear of the face when unrelated men are near is widely permitted.
  • On menstruation. She can travel, enter ihram, make the intention and say the talbiyah, and do everything except pray and perform tawaf. Since tawaf is a pillar, the Umrah simply waits until she's able. If her cycle is likely to fall across Day 4, that's a conversation to have with her own physician months ahead, not weeks — suppression regimens need lead time to be tolerated, and starting one a few days out generally causes breakthrough bleeding. The alternative is to stay in ihram and complete the Umrah later in the week, which is exactly why four nights in Makkah rather than three is the right call.

If you slip up

Accidental violations — a scented soap, a forgotten cap — generally carry an expiation rather than invalidating anything, and what's owed depends on what happened and whether you knew. Don't spiral about it, and don't take rulings from other pilgrims standing next to you in a queue. There are staffed guidance desks inside both mosques with scholars who answer exactly these questions, in English, all day.

The places, and why they matter المواضع

Short version of what each site actually is, so you're not standing in front of something significant with no idea why.

In Madinah

  • Masjid an-Nabawi — the Prophet's Mosque. He built the original in 622 CE when he migrated here from Makkah; his own house adjoined it, and he was buried in that room, which successive expansions eventually enclosed within the mosque. The famous green dome is over it. The mosque now holds hundreds of thousands.
  • The Rawdah — the small strip between his grave and the pulpit he preached from, which he described as a garden of Paradise. Green carpet marks it out from the red carpet everywhere else. Physically unremarkable and about the size of a tennis court, which is why access is rationed.
  • Jannat al-Baqi — Madinah's main cemetery since the Prophet's lifetime. Thousands of Companions, several of his wives and children. The graves are deliberately unmarked and level, to prevent them becoming shrines. Men only, and only in the windows after Fajr and Asr.
  • Masjid Quba — the first mosque in Islam, built the week the Prophet ﷺ arrived. Two units of prayer here, after washing at home, carry the reward of an Umrah.
  • Uhud — the mountain outside the city where the second major battle was fought in 625 CE. Seventy Companions died, including the Prophet's uncle Hamza. Archers' Hill, where a unit abandoned its position and turned a victory into a defeat, is one of the most-taught episodes in Islamic history.
  • Masjid al-Qiblatayn — "the mosque of the two directions." Muslims originally prayed toward Jerusalem; revelation changed the direction to Makkah, and it arrived during a congregational prayer here, so the whole congregation turned around mid-prayer.

In Makkah

  • Masjid al-Haram — the mosque around the Kaaba, and the largest in the world. Multiple levels, dozens of numbered gates, and a permanent state of construction.
  • Jabal an-Nur — "the mountain of light," holding the cave of Hira where the Prophet ﷺ used to retreat and where the first verses of the Qur'an were revealed to him. The climb is steep and long; there is no ritual attached to it.
  • Mina, Muzdalifah, Arafat — the three sites where Hajj takes place. Empty and eerie outside the season. Arafat in particular, a wide plain where two million people stand together for one afternoon a year, is worth driving through even though it has nothing to do with Umrah.
  • Masjid Aisha (Tan'eem) — the nearest boundary point for anyone already inside Makkah who wants to enter ihram for another Umrah. Purely functional.

Things you'll see that aren't part of it تنبيه

You will watch people touch and rub the walls of the Kaaba and the mosque for blessing, pray facing specific pillars, collect earth from Uhud, tie things to railings, or perform sequences nobody can explain. Some of this is cultural, some of it is folk practice, and some of it is actively discouraged. None of it is required and none of it is your problem.

Do the four steps. Pray. Say what you came to say. Ignore the rest, and don't take instruction from confident strangers — a striking amount of bad information circulates in those crowds.

The reverse also applies: nobody is watching you. Everyone there is preoccupied, most people are also doing it for the first time, and the staff have seen every possible mistake. Ask a guidance desk if you're stuck. You will not be the most lost person in the building.

Where to stay السكن

Madinah — mid-range, and still on the doorstep

  • Anwar Al Madinah Mövenpick — directly on the southern piazza, arguably the best location in the city. Ask for a courtyard-facing room.
  • Dar Al Taqwa — a short walk to the main gates, quieter, well run, popular with couples.
  • Frontel Al Harithia or Millennium Al Aqeeq — a few minutes further out, noticeably cheaper, still an easy walk for Fajr.

Makkah — this is where the money goes

  • Address Jabal Omar — the strongest choice for a couple. Resort standard, calm, and a covered five-minute walk down to the King Abdulaziz Gate. Not inside the clock tower, which is a feature.
  • Raffles Makkah Palace — all suites, butler service, inside the Abraj al-Bait complex with covered access straight into the Haram. The most sheltered option there is.
  • Conrad Makkah or Jabal Omar Hyatt Regency — same Jabal Omar side, a step down in price, still a comfortable walk.
  • Fairmont Makkah Clock Royal Tower — only if a Kaaba-view room is genuinely what you want. It's spectacular and it's expensive, and you'll be inside the mosque anyway.

Whatever you book, confirm it's licensed and registered on Nusuk Masar before you pay — the visa depends on it.

Practical تفاصيل

  • Shoes. You remove them to enter either mosque. Carry a cloth bag rather than using the racks, which fill up and are unnumbered — and always know which gate you came in by.
  • Marble burns. Between roughly 10am and 4pm the open courtyards are genuinely too hot for bare feet. Cross in the shade or stay inside.
  • Water. Drink far more than feels necessary. Zamzam is free everywhere in both mosques.
  • Separate sections. Men and women pray in different areas of both mosques. Agree where to meet afterwards before you go in. During tawaf itself the mataf is mixed.
  • Mahram. As her husband you're her mahram, so none of the guardianship rules that complicate solo female pilgrims apply to you.
  • Cards work almost everywhere. Carry a couple of hundred riyals for taxis, barbers and the date souq.
  • Careem beats Uber locally; both work.
  • Crowd illness. Respiratory bugs move fast through the Haram in season — it's common enough to have a nickname. Masks in the mataf, hand hygiene, and pack analgesia, antihistamine, ORS, blister plasters and throat lozenges.
  • Feet. You'll do 15–20k steps a day on marble. Break the sandals in at home. Use blister plasters early rather than heroically.
  • Phones. Full charge every morning and a power bank each. Your permits, tickets and boarding passes all live there.
  • Photos. Fine in the courtyards, discouraged inside and around the mataf. Put it away and just be there.
  • Rain. November is one of the wetter months on this coast. Unlikely, not impossible.

Pack for ihram specifically

  • Two sets of ihram sheets for him — you'll want a clean spare
  • An ihram belt with pockets for phone and cards
  • Unscented soap, shampoo, deodorant, wipes and lip balm, bought at home, in their own bag
  • Flat sandals with the instep exposed, already broken in
  • Two loose plain abayas or equivalent for her, plus a light shawl
  • A small folding prayer mat each
