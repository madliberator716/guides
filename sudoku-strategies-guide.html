Sudoku strategies guide

Twelve techniques, from the ones you use on every puzzle to the ones you only need on the hardest. Each has a picture and a plain-English walkthrough.

The idea behind everything

Every row, column, and 3 by 3 box needs the digits 1 to 9 exactly once. Each digit already placed rules that digit out for every cell that shares a row, column, or box with it. Every strategy below is just a smarter way of ruling digits out until one fits.

The solving loop
  1. Scan for digits and place any singles (strategies 1 to 3).
  2. Stuck? Write pencil marks in every empty cell (strategy 4).
  3. Look for pairs and triples (5, 6), then pointing and box-line patterns (7, 8).
  4. Still stuck? Try X-Wing, Swordfish, XY-Wing, and coloring (9 to 12).
  5. After any placement or elimination, go back to step 1.

Basic

1. Scan rows, columns, boxes

Pick one digit and look at every place it has already been written. A 5 in a row means no other cell in that row can be a 5. The same goes for its column and its box. Do this for every placed 5, then look for a box, row, or column where only one cell is left that can hold a 5.

Reading the picture The four white cells are placed 5s. Red cells can't hold a 5 because they share a row or column with one: rows 1 and 2, and columns 8 and 9. In the outlined top-right box, every cell is blocked except row 3, column 7. The box still needs a 5, so it goes there (green).
Use it when: always, and first. Cycle through the digits 1 to 9. Start with digits that already appear many times, because they block the most cells.

Common slip: forgetting that a placed digit also blocks its own box, not just its row and column. Check all three.

Basic

2. Naked single

A cell with only one possible digit left. To check a cell, list the digits already in its row, column, and box. If eight different digits show up, the ninth goes in the cell.

Reading the picture This is one box with its candidates written in. The 1, 3, 6, and 8 are already placed. The middle-left cell started with more options, but its row and column have already ruled out 2, 5, 7, and 9. Only 4 is left, so it goes in (green).
Use it when: a row, column, or box is nearly full, or after you've written pencil marks and a cell shows just one digit.
Basic

3. Hidden single

A digit that fits in only one cell of a row, column, or box, even if that cell has other candidates too. The cell is hiding the answer among its extra candidates.

Reading the picture The top-middle cell lists 2, 5, and 7. Look at the rest of the box: no other empty cell can be a 7. The box needs a 7 somewhere, so this cell must be it. Once you place the 7, the 2 and 5 in that cell are erased.
Use it when: you have pencil marks and no naked singles. Take one digit at a time and count how many cells in a unit still list it. If the count is one, place it. Scanning (strategy 1) is hidden singles done without pencil marks.
Basic

4. Pencil marks

Small candidate digits written in each empty cell. They turn invisible patterns into visible ones, and every strategy from 5 onward depends on them. To fill them in: for each empty cell, start with 1 to 9, then cross off every digit already in its row, column, or box.

Reading the picture The box already has 1, 3, 6, and 8, so it still needs 2, 4, 5, 7, and 9. Each empty cell lists which of those it could still be. Notice how much easier it is to spot the 4 and the 7 from this view than from the bare grid.
Use it when: scanning stalls. Write marks once, carefully. After that, every time you place a digit, erase it from the marks in its row, column, and box.

Common slip: writing marks too early on an easy puzzle (wasted effort), or forgetting to update them after placing a digit.

Intermediate

5. Naked pairs and triples

If two cells in the same unit have only the same two candidates, those cells must hold those two digits between them. No other cell in the unit can use either digit, so erase them. A naked triple is the same idea with three cells and three digits.

Naked pair

Reading the picture The two blue cells can only be 2 or 5. So the 2 and 5 are spoken for. The cell next to them, {2, 5, 7}, loses both and becomes a 7. The other two cells lose their 2 and 5 as well, and both end up as {1, 9}, which is a new naked pair. One pair often sets off a chain.

Naked triple

Reading the picture The three blue cells use only the digits 1, 4, and 7 between them. Each cell does not need to contain all three. Together they claim 1, 4, and 7, so those digits are erased from the rest of the row. The two remaining cells collapse to 9 and 8.
Use it when: you have pencil marks and no singles. Scan each unit for cells with two or three candidates, and look for matches.
Intermediate

6. Hidden pairs

Two digits that appear as candidates in only the same two cells of a unit. Those two cells must hold those two digits, so every other candidate in them can be erased. It is a naked pair in disguise.

Reading the picture Look at the digits 3 and 8. Across the whole row, they appear only in the two blue cells. So those cells must be the 3 and the 8. The 5 in the first blue cell and the 1 in the second are erased, leaving a clean pair of {3, 8}.
Use it when: a pair of digits seems to show up in just two cells of a row, column, or box. Count the cells for each digit and see if two digits share the same two cells.
Intermediate

7. Pointing pairs and triples

If every candidate for a digit inside one box lies in a single row (or column), the box's copy of that digit must be in that line. So no cell of that line outside the box can hold the digit. Two cells in a line make a pointing pair, three make a pointing triple.

Reading the picture This grid shows every cell that could still be a 4. In the outlined top-left box, all the candidates for 4 are in row 2 (blue). The box needs a 4, so it will land in row 2. That means the rest of row 2 can't have a 4. The red cells are erased.
Use it when: you have pencil marks. Look at one digit inside one box at a time and see whether its candidates line up.
Intermediate

8. Box-line reduction

The reverse of pointing. If every candidate for a digit in a row (or column) lies inside one box, then that box's copy of the digit must be in that line. So the other cells of the box can't hold the digit.

Reading the picture In row 5, the only cells that can be a 6 are the two blue ones, and both sit in the outlined center box. Row 5 needs a 6, so the center box's 6 is in row 5. The other cells in that box (the red ones, in rows 4 and 6) can't be 6.
Use it when: a row or column has only two or three candidates for a digit and they all sit in the same box. Pointing and box-line are mirror images, so check both directions.
Advanced

9. X-Wing

Pick a digit. If it can go in exactly two cells in each of two rows, and those cells are in the same two columns, you have an X-Wing. The four cells form a rectangle. Erase the digit from everywhere else in those two columns.

Reading the picture In rows 2 and 7, the digit 6 can only go in columns 3 and 8 (blue rectangle). There are just two ways to place those 6s: top-left with bottom-right, or top-right with bottom-left. Either way, columns 3 and 8 each get their 6 from rows 2 and 7. So every other 6 candidate in those columns (red) is erased.
Use it when: basic and intermediate methods are exhausted. Scan for a digit with exactly two candidates in a row, then look for another row with the same two columns. It also works with rows and columns swapped.
Advanced

10. Swordfish

X-Wing stretched to three rows and three columns. If a digit's candidates in three rows all sit inside the same three columns, those three rows will use up all three columns. Erase the digit from the rest of those columns.

Reading the picture For the digit 3, rows 1, 4, and 8 can only place it in columns 2, 5, and 7. The six blue cells form a loop. Each row has two candidates here, but a row may have two or three in the pattern. The three 3s must fall in three different columns, so columns 2, 5, and 7 each get a 3 from those rows. The red cells are erased.
Use it when: X-Wing finds nothing. Look for three rows where a digit has two or three candidates, all within three columns. Rare, but it does show up on hard puzzles.
Advanced

11. XY-Wing

Three cells that each have exactly two candidates, linked in a chain. One pivot cell and two wing cells share digits so that one of the wings is guaranteed to hold a particular digit. Any cell that sees both wings can't hold that digit.

Reading the picture The pivot is {2, 6}. Wing A is {2, 9} and shares a row with the pivot. Wing B is {6, 9} and shares a column with it. If the pivot is 2, Wing A can't be 2, so it's 9. If the pivot is 6, Wing B can't be 6, so it's 9. Either way, one of the wings is a 9. The target cell sees both wings (down Wing A's column and along Wing B's row), so it can't be 9. Its 9 is struck out.
Use it when: you're stuck and have pencil marks. Find a cell with exactly two candidates, then look for two more two-candidate cells it can see, each sharing one digit with it and a third digit in common with each other.
Advanced

12. Coloring

Focus on one digit. Wherever a row, column, or box has exactly two candidates for that digit, one of them must be true and the other false. Link those pairs into a chain and color it in alternating colors, A, B, A, B. Every cell of one color is true and every cell of the other is false, so you know the cells move together.

Reading the picture For the digit 7, row 1 has only two candidates (top row), column 8 has only two, and row 6 has only two. The chain runs A, B, A, B across them. If either A cell is a 7, both are, and both B cells aren't. If a B cell is the 7, the reverse holds. Either way, one color is true. The red cell sees an A cell above it in its column and a B cell in its box, so it sees a true 7 no matter what. It can't be 7 itself.
Use it when: everything else has failed. A second rule: if two cells of the same color see each other, that color must be false, so every cell of the other color is a 7.

Habits that help

  • Work one digit at a time, then switch.
  • Recheck after every placement. Each one can unlock others.
  • Move to harder techniques only when the easier ones stall.
  • Don't guess. If you can't find a move, you're missing a candidate or a pattern, not a lucky digit.
  • If a puzzle suddenly makes no sense, an earlier mark was wrong. Recheck your pencil marks before anything else.
